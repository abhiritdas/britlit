Although the texts portray happiness in different ways, they collectively suggest that true happiness comes less from wealth, status, or external circumstances but rather from one's inner perspective, love, and hope; however, happiness is not equally available to everyone because poverty and oppression can severely limit a person's ability to experience it.

Several of the texts suggest that happiness depends less on external circumstances than how a person understands and responds to those circumstances. In *Paradise Lost*, Milton writes that “the mind is its own place” and can transform Heaven into Hell or Hell into Heaven. This suggests that a person's perspective has the power to shape their experience of life. Shakespeare similarly presents happiness as something that cannot be measured by wealth or social position in *Sonnet 29*. Although the speaker initially feels miserable because he envies people who are richer, more talented, and more social, remembering the person he loves changed his attitude that he would not exchange his situation with people of privilege. Love allows him to recognize that emotional fulfillment is more valuable than status. Gray presents a similar idea in *Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard*, where the poor villagers live away from the conflicts of society and maintain a peaceful way of life. Their happiness comes from their modest and peaceful lives rather than ambition or wealth. These texts suggest that happiness can be found when people value inner peace, love, and contentment instead of comparing themselves to others.

The earlier poems treat happiness as a change in perspective. William Blake’s two poems called “The Chimney Sweeper,” however, show why perspective alone is not always enough. In the poem from *Songs of Innocence*, Tom Dacre dreams that an angel promises he will “never want joy” if he is good. When he wakes up, nothing around him has changed. He and the other boys still rise in the dark and go to work with their bags and brushes. The narrator calls Tom “happy and warm,” even though the morning is cold. That contrast makes his happiness seem uncertain. The dream may comfort him, but it cannot change the life he wakes up to. The poem from *Songs of Experience* makes this problem harder to ignore. A different child says that his parents think they have done him “no injury” because he can still “dance and sing.” He sees his life differently, accusing “God and His priest and king” of making “a heaven of our misery.” His words show how easily adults can mistake a child’s cheerful behavior for proof that everything is fine. Blake does not present hope as useless; Tom’s dream may help him face the morning. Still, the poems show that hope and happiness are not the same. Hope can make suffering easier to bear, but it cannot remove what causes it.

Wordsworth expands this idea by showing that happiness can survive even when the immediate experience of joy has faded. In “Lines Composed a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey,” Wordsworth explains that his youthful excitement in nature has passed, but the memories of that experience continue to provide him with “healing thoughts” during periods of solitude, fear, pain, or grief. Nature therefore gives him a form of happiness that becomes deeper and more reflective with age rather than disappearing completely. His happiness is no longer based simply on the excitement of experiencing nature in the moment, but on his ability to remember and interpret those experiences later in life. This suggests that happiness can remain available even during difficult circumstances when people have meaningful memories and relationships to draw upon. However, this kind of happiness also depends on having access to experiences that can provide comfort and reflection, which further suggests that happiness is influenced by the opportunities a person has throughout life.

The texts also suggest that happiness can be sustained through imagination and hope, even when the present world is painful. In Shelley’s “Ode to the West Wind,” the speaker asks the wind to carry his words across the world and hopes that his poetry will create a “new birth.” Although the speaker describes himself as burdened by age, weakness, and sadness, he imagines a future in which his words can produce change and renewal. His happiness therefore comes not from his current circumstances but from the possibility that something better can emerge from them. This idea is similar to Blake’s presentation of Tom’s hope for a better future, but Shelley places the source of hope in human creativity rather than religious obedience. Both texts show that hope can make suffering more bearable, but they also reveal its limitations: hope does not immediately remove the conditions that cause suffering. Happiness, therefore, is possible even in an imperfect world, but whether a person can experience it depends not only on their ability to change their perspective, but also on whether they have love, meaningful memories, imagination, or hope to help them endure the circumstances around them.
